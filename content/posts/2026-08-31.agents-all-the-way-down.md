![Agents All the Way Down](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-01.png)

Earlier this year I was at Disney World with my family. I don't remember whether we were walking around Magic Kingdom or already in line for [Tiana's Bayou Adventure](https://disneyworld.disney.go.com/attractions/magic-kingdom/tianas-bayou-adventure/) when a message from work showed up on my phone, but I know I was nowhere near my laptop.

![One hunting lease contract had failed. Twice. "Boundary map not found."](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-02.png)

At work, our lease managers use one of our applications to send hunting lease contracts to an outside contract service. Someone from our team messaged me because one of those contracts had failed twice. The application said it couldn't find one of the maps it needed to build the contract.

![Maps generated -> Network stored -> S3 every 30 mins -> Lease app builds contract](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-03.png)

That error usually means exactly what it says.

Our land information services team generates the maps and puts them on the network. Then I have a cron job that runs every thirty minutes and copies them up to [S3](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonS3/latest/userguide/Welcome.html), where our lease application can read them.

Some of our lease managers, I'll admit, are a little trigger-happy. They'll create a tract, put it on a lease, and try to send the contract before any of that has happened. I had seen that plenty of times before, so that was my first guess.

![The map should have been there.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-04.png)

I checked the audit log from my phone. The timing suggested the map should already have made it into the application, so my obvious explanation didn't fit. I didn't have a better one yet.

![Two-person development team](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-05.png)

We're a two-person development team. We work with contractors too, but getting one of them into a production bug isn't immediate. Unfortunately, our other developer was unavailable.

I had set up [Codex remote mode](https://openai.com/index/work-with-codex-from-anywhere/) on my Mac mini right before I left for vacation, but I hadn't used it on a real bug yet.

So I figured, [why the hell not](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GI_fvDOUCu8).

I pasted in the [Teams](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-teams/group-chat-software) thread, the error, what I had found in the audit log, and my basic idea of what might be happening. Then I asked it to investigate on a new branch and see if it could get us to a small fix.

![Codex came back with two changes. One looked like the bug.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-06.png)

It went off and churned for a few minutes, then came back with two changes and some tests. One looked like a useful fix around the map lookup. The second sounded like the bug we were actually chasing. Both patches were small, and the diff looked fine.

![The first test didn't pass my smell test. Real database state. As little mocking as the integration allowed.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-07.png)

Codex couldn't run our test suite because this was a fairly new Mac mini and I hadn't installed [Docker](https://docs.docker.com/get-started/docker-overview/) yet.

The tests it wrote didn't pass my smell test, either. Any third-party integration requires some amount of mocking, but agents left to their own devices will mock endlessly.

I pushed back and asked for a more realistic regression test without bending ourselves into knots or excessive mocking. It wrote one that kept a stale object in memory, changed the real state in the database, and then showed that the background job reloaded the object before sending the contract. That looked much more like the bug we were trying to fix, so I asked Codex to push everything up.

![CI passed. The contract went through.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-08.png)

I opened a pull request and let CI do its thing. It passed. I gave the diff one more once-over in the [GitHub app](https://github.com/mobile) as a sanity check, then merged it.

I knew that if the patch didn't fix the issue, we would be in the same place we were already in. I also knew it wasn't going to bring down production. The lease manager tried again, and the contract went through.

![20 minutes, in pieces, from the line vs. hours back at the hotel.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-09.png)

The Codex app felt new and surprisingly polished, but the process was what I normally did at my laptop: think through the problem, guide the agent, push back where I needed to, wait for CI, and decide whether to merge. I spent maybe twenty minutes on it in pieces while I stayed in line with my family, instead of losing a few hours of our vacation back at the hotel.

![The process took six years to build.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-10.png)

Codex moved that work onto my phone. The process I was following had taken me six years to build.

## The six years behind the merge

![Hi, I'm Josh.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-11.png)

Hi, I'm Josh Thomas. I'm the lead developer at [The Westervelt Company](https://westervelt.com/about/westervelt-history/), a land and lumber company in Alabama that's been around for more than 140 years.

![~10 Django applications, oldest 19 years the lease application](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-12.png)

We have about ten [Django](https://www.djangoproject.com/start/overview/) applications covering everything from mill safety to mineral payments and bookings for our hunting lodge. The lease application from the Disney story is the biggest. It manages leases across hundreds of thousands of acres, and it's nineteen years old.

![For a while, I was the whole development team: developer, designer, QA, DevOps, product, user support](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-13.png)

I've been a professional developer for six years, all of them at Westervelt, and this was my first job writing software for a living.

For a while I was the whole development team. I was the developer, designer, QA, DevOps, product manager, and the person talking to our users.

I was also learning all of those jobs while I was doing them.

![Bitbucket -> ZIP file -> Windows + IIS](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-14.png)

The software I inherited was a mixture. We had [WordPress](https://wordpress.org/about/history/) and [ColdFusion](https://helpx.adobe.com/coldfusion/developing-applications/user-guide.html) applications built inside the company that weren't in version control.

We also had applications built by contractors. Those were in [Bitbucket](https://www.atlassian.com/git/tutorials/what-is-bitbucket), but when the contractors needed to deploy one, they sent us a zip file. We copied it onto a [Windows server](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/get-started/windows-server-overview) and restarted [IIS](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/iis/get-started/introduction-to-iis/iis-web-server-overview).

![I build the development loop I thought we needed. GitHub organization, tests -> CI -> automated deployment](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-15.png)

So I did I thought what any normal developer should do. I created a GitHub organization, got access to the contractor repositories, moved them into our account, and started the hard work of bringing in tests, CI, and automated deployments.

None of that was in my job description, and no one had asked me to do it. It was how I understood modern software should be built, and my boss gave me room to do it.

![Deploying became merging a pull request I trusted. That was leverage.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-16.png)

My first [GitHub Actions workflows](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/get-started/understand-github-actions) were overengineered and sometimes flaky, but the goal was simple. I was new, responsible for all of it, and wanted enough evidence around a change that I could sleep after deploying it.

Once tests and deployment moved into CI, deploying became merging a pull request I trusted. That was leverage: the same work still happened, but I no longer had to carry every step myself.

![A useful tool had to take real work off my plate.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-17.png)

I learned most of this job while doing it and by reading a lot of open-source code. I would not be standing here without open source.

By the time the first AI coding tools showed up, I had a pretty clear standard for a useful tool: if I was going to make room for it in my day, it needed to take some real work off my plate.

![ChatGPT, copy and paste. Copilot, half measure. Tab completion, interrupted thought](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-18.png)

[ChatGPT](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt/) in the browser required too much copying back and forth. [Copilot](https://github.blog/news-insights/product-news/introducing-github-copilot-ai-pair-programmer/) and tab completion sat closer to the project, but they interrupted my flow without taking enough work off my plate.

![Aider, inside the project. Claude Code, kept the loop working](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-19.png)

Then I tried [Aider](https://aider.chat/). Does anybody here remember Aider? It was doing the [Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/overview) thing before Claude Code: running in the terminal, reading and editing files, and working inside the project.

Claude Code went further by finding its own context, running commands, reading the result, and continuing. These tools could finally take a task through the development loop I had already built.

![CI, moved repeatable work out of my hands. Agents, take the first pass at work that isn't repeatable.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-20.png)

The connection, for me, was leverage. CI had moved repeatable work out of my hands. An agent could take the first pass at work that wasn't repeatable.

It could take a bug report, inspect the project, and bring me back a possible cause before I had given the rest of my day to it. Even when the diagnosis was wrong, it could rule something out or give me a place to start.

That's what happened at Disney. I spent about twenty minutes of my own attention instead of a few hours back at the hotel. I stayed with my family, and the contract still went out.

![CI is deterministic. An agent isn't.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-21.png)

But those are two very different kinds of leverage. CI is supposed to be deterministic. An agent isn't.

That unpredictability is part of why it can [investigate a problem and come up with a novel solution](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/competitive-programming-with-alphacode/). It's also part of why I can't trust it the way I trust the test runner.

![The tests can pass while the data model is still wrong.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-22.png)

CI can tell me the tests passed. The tests can all pass while the data model is still wrong.

## What agents changed about my Django code

![What agents changed about my Django code](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-23.png)

The place this has changed my own Django code the most is in the models. I've started paying much more attention to whether the business relationships are actually visible there, or whether they only make sense after someone explains them.

!["Go with the grain of Django." - Carlton Gibson](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-24.png)

[Carlton Gibson](https://noumenal.es/) has this saying:

> Go with the grain of Django.[^carlton-grain]

[^carlton-grain]: Carlton has not, as far as I can find, said those exact words.

    He has [written that “Django has a grain” and advised readers to “lean into it”](https://buttondown.com/carlton/archive/evolving-djangos-authuser/), and [described HTMX as working “with the grain of the framework”](https://talkpython.fm/episodes/show/428/django-trends-in-2023#transcript_at_3777). I had compressed that idea into a sentence and mistakenly remembered it as a direct quote. 

    *Mea culpa*, this was one of the last things I added to the talk and I didn't do my due diligence finding exactly what he had said regarding the grain of Django.

    Though after posting about this on Mastodon earlier, Carlton *did* actually end up [posting the quote](https://chaos.social/@carlton/117191429564984827), so maybe I'm just out here manifesting my reality.

Django has twenty years of conventions around how its applications are laid out and how its pieces fit together. There are twenty years of documentation, tutorials, [Stack Overflow answers](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/tagged/django?tab=Votes), and open-source projects.

Django itself is in the training data, and it's one of the main projects used in the [benchmark suites](https://www.swebench.com/SWE-bench/) the labs use to test coding agents. Agents know Django very well. The farther an application gets from it, the more context I have to supply.

![Some disagreements are just taste. My preference: Manager + QuerySet. Agent preference: services.py](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-25.png)

Going against the grain isn't automatically wrong, though. For example, I tend to put my service-layer code in custom model managers and querysets. To me, [that feels like going with the grain of Django](https://www.b-list.org/weblog/2020/mar/16/no-service/).

Agents love creating a `services.py` or a separate service module instead. [I'm sure plenty of people in this room prefer that.](https://www.jamesbeith.co.uk/blog/how-to-structure-django-projects/)

I still think my way is right, but both can produce clear code, and fighting the agent over it every session doesn't buy me much. Django has a grain, and agents have a grain too. I've learned to loosen my grip when the difference is mostly taste.

!["This bool could've been an enum." - Charlie Marsh](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-26.png)

Taste still carries information. Charlie Marsh, who created Ruff, uv, ty, and is now an employee of OpenAI I guess, has posted the [same](https://x.com/charliermarsh/status/1811937520541663397?s=20) [joke](https://x.com/charliermarsh/status/2069796668506042584?s=20) twice[^charlie-marsh-callout]:

[^charlie-marsh-callout]: Sorry for the callout Charlie, big fan!

> This bool could've been an enum.

A boolean makes the surrounding code explain what true and false mean. An enum names the values and has somewhere to put a third state. That is partly taste, but I think it's useful taste.

![The lease application carried nineteen years of design with it. Access -> ColdFusion + SQL -> Django](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-27.png)

The lease application had a much bigger modeling problem. The application started as an [Access database](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/access/database-design-basics) nineteen years ago, moved through a ColdFusion application written mostly with raw SQL, and eventually became Django.

The older design used fairly flat tables. At the time that made sense. Direct SQL and flat rows can be simple. After nineteen years of features and changes, some of the business relationships were just hard to see.

![Lease -> Tracts A, B, and C. One tract is primary.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-28.png)

A hunting lease can cover several tracts of land, and one of them is the primary tract. Other parts of the business use that tract to identify the lease and connect it to the landowner.

![Before: is primary field on a tract.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-29.png)

The old model stored `is_primary` as a boolean on each tract assignment. If a lease covered five tracts, there were five rows, and one of them was supposed to say true.

We later added a constraint so no more than one active tract for the same club and year could be primary. That helped, but the `Lease` itself still had no relationship to follow. To find its primary tract, the code had to work back through the club, the year, the active status, and the tract assignments.

![Person: learns the rule once. Agents: reconstructs it every session.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-30.png)

A person onboarding to the codebase could learn that rule once and remember it. An agent wakes up every session having to relearn the entire world. Every time one touched this part of the application, I had to make sure it had reconstructed that same answer in its context.

![I already knew the model was awkward. Managers, QuerySets, annotations, aggregations. Reports and Power BI read the tables directly.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-31.png)

I had known the model was awkward for years. I got very good at writing [custom managers](https://docs.djangoproject.com/en/6.1/topics/db/managers/), [querysets](https://docs.djangoproject.com/en/6.1/topics/db/managers/), [annotations](https://docs.djangoproject.com/en/6.1/ref/models/querysets/#django.db.models.query.QuerySet.annotate), and [aggregations](https://docs.djangoproject.com/en/6.1/topics/db/aggregation/) to smooth over those inherited relationships. That's actually where [my first DjangoCon talk](https://2023.djangocon.us/talks/custom-model-managers-and-querysets-graduating-from-django-beginner-to-orm-master/) came from.

Changing the model was not impossible, but reports and [Power BI integrations](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/power-bi/fundamentals/power-bi-overview) read those tables directly. A schema change meant coordinating with people outside the application, which was enough friction for the workaround to keep winning. It was easier to teach a human developer the rule once and let them keep moving.

The refactor was already on my list. Having to teach the same strange rule to every new agent session was what finally pushed me to do it.

![After: primary tract field on lease.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-32.png)

We added `primary_tract` as a [foreign key](https://docs.djangoproject.com/en/6.1/topics/db/examples/many_to_one/) on `Lease` and wrote a data migration for the old rows. It only set `primary_tract` when it could find a matching active tract, rather than guessing, so the new field is still nullable.

Some old lookup behavior remains for the same reason. The model is not perfect, and the database does not prevent every invalid state.

![lease.primary_tract, one obvious place to begin](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-33.png)

But now the first answer to “what is this lease's primary tract?” is `lease.primary_tract`. The relationship is written down where you would expect to find it: on the lease.

Now an agent can find that relationship without as much explanation. That also helps the next person who works on the application, including me six months down the road.

![What kind of disagreement is this? A harmless preference? An information-poor representation? A domain fact the application should state?](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-34.png)

More generally, when an agent keeps getting something wrong, I ask myself what kind of disagreement we're having. Is it fighting a harmless preference, like where I put a service function? Is it struggling with an information-poor representation, like a boolean? Or is it repeatedly reconstructing a fact the application should state directly?

![Domain fact: model, relationship, constraint. Known smell: lint rule or system check. Taste: sometimes let it go](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-35.png)

If it's a domain fact, I look for a model, a relationship, or a [constraint](https://docs.djangoproject.com/en/6.1/ref/models/constraints/) that can say it. If it's a code smell I can identify mechanically, maybe it becomes a [lint rule](https://ast-grep.github.io/guide/rule-config.html) or a [Django system check](https://docs.djangoproject.com/en/6.1/topics/checks/). If it's just taste, I've learned to sometimes let it go.

![Put the business rule in the code. Not in the next prompt.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-36.png)

When I find myself typing the same business rule into prompt after prompt, I don't want to solve that with a better prompt. I want the next agent to find the rule in the code.

None of this means the agent will choose the better data structure on its own. When the code leaves room for an easy answer, it usually takes it.

## Work costs nothing to an LLM

![Slop: code that looks plausible and solves the problem on the surface. It may pass every test. It may work in production.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-37.png)

Slop is the word everybody uses for bad agent code now. What I mean by it here is code that looks plausible and solves the problem sitting on the surface. It may pass every test. It may even work in production. But it solves the problem in front of it without dealing with the larger problem underneath.

Earlier this year Bryan Cantrill wrote a post called [“The Peril of Laziness Lost”](https://bcantrill.dtrace.org/2026/04/12/the-peril-of-laziness-lost/). It starts with [Larry Wall's three virtues of a programmer](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/programming-perl-4th/9781449321451/ch24s03.html): laziness, impatience, and hubris. Cantrill argues that laziness is the most important one.

We do hard design work now because we don't want ourselves or the next person to keep paying for the same awkward design later.

!["Work costs nothing to an LLM." - Bryan Cantrill](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-38.png)

His shortest version of what agents lack is this:

> Work costs nothing to an LLM.

An agent never has to maintain the two hundred lines it just added. It never gets tired of working around the same bad interface. It doesn't feel the codebase getting harder to hold in its head, because it doesn't have to hold that codebase in its head tomorrow.

It doesn't have a head.

![Local fix -> Green tests -> New pattern. The next agent copies it.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-39.png)

So it takes the local fix. If the tests fail, it changes the code until they pass. If writing a pile of mocks gets them to pass, it will write the pile of mocks.

And because the agent can write both the implementation and the tests, it can write tests that prove the solution it already chose.

Once that solution is in the codebase, it becomes a fact for the next agent.[^codebase-as-prompt] Agents are much more likely to accept an existing pattern than to stop and ask whether that pattern should exist in the first place.

[^codebase-as-prompt]: Josh Bleecher Snyder writes, “[Prompts come and go; your code endures. Agents mirror local style. Your codebase is the prompt.](https://commaok.xyz/ai/codebase-as-prompt/)”

    Thorsten Ball describes the negative version of the same feedback loop in [_Joy & Curiosity #81_](https://registerspill.thorstenball.com/p/joy-and-curiosity-81): the next agent sees the previous agent's defensive checks and concludes that “in this codebase anything's possible really.”

**Slop compounds.** A small bad choice gets copied into the next feature, and the next one, until it becomes how the application works.

![An old application gives the agent mixed evidence. Deliberate, inherited, too risky to change: the agent sees all three as equally real.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-40.png)

An old application gives the agent even more mixed evidence. The lease app contains choices made at different times by different people for different reasons. Some are deliberate. Some are inherited. Some remain because changing them would break something.

An agent sees all of them as equally real. When the code gives it mixed answers, it tends to reach for the most common answer it has seen elsewhere.

![5 lines, the original fix -> 200 lines, after adversarial review.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-41.png)

I have tried using more agents to solve this. I'll send an implementation through adversarial reviewers.

That helps until the implementing agent treats every criticism as an order. A reviewer told to find complexity will find some, and a five-line fix can become two hundred lines of defensive checks for things that are unlikely or impossible.

![Tests, known behavior. Linters, repeated patterns. CI, runs the checks. Reviewers, another option. Human review decides whether the design fits.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-42.png)

The reviewer has the same missing judgment as the implementer. It gives me another opinion without telling me how much that opinion matters.

So I treat it as one more check: tests catch behavior we knew to test, linters catch patterns we already rejected, CI runs those checks every time, and reviewers may catch something another agent missed. Human review is still where I decide whether the design fits the application.

![Review depth follows risk. One-off script: glance and run. Scheduled job: read more. Core model: close review.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-43.png)

How carefully I read depends on what the change can hurt. If an agent writes a one-off script that only I will run, I may glance at the shape, run it, and see what happens. If that script will run every day as a scheduled job, I read a little more. If it changes a core model or a path our users depend on, I read it with a fine-tooth comb.

![The Disney patch was safe enough for that incident. The deeper redesign is still waiting.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-44.png)

That was how I looked at the Disney patch. I knew the code, the patch was small, and CI passed. If it failed, the contract would still be stuck; I could see it would not bring down production.

I could not reconsider the whole integration from my phone, and I still have not done that deeper redesign. What I established from Disney was that the patch was safe enough for that incident.

![Three years in, the failure mode remains. Some weeks are genuinely fun, other wees the same corrections again.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-45.png)

Maybe one day I can stop reading the code. Current agents have not earned that from me. After about three years, this failure mode has not gone away. Some weeks working with them is genuinely fun; other weeks I correct the same mistakes until I wonder why I am doing this.

![Less anxious about the queue. More annoyed at the agent.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-46.png)

Then I compare that annoyance with how work used to feel. A bug report would land on an already-full plate and displace something else.

Now an agent can do the first investigation and give me some idea of the cause and size before I decide how much attention it needs. I spend less time anxious about everything waiting for me and more time annoyed at the agent in front of me. So far, I can live with that trade.

## What remains mine

![What remains mine](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-47.png)

One reason I keep making that trade is that agents have changed what I can afford to try in my spare time.

![Django language server. First version: Python tag + dedicated TOML rules. Current version: Python source -> AST -> validation.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-48.png)

I've been working on a [Django language server](https://github.com/joshuadavidthomas/django-language-server). Django normally finds a bad template tag when the template renderer runs; a language server needs to find it while you're still editing.

[One version](https://github.com/joshuadavidthomas/djtagspecs) duplicated each tag's validation rules in TOML. The version I'm working on now scans the [Python AST](https://docs.python.org/3/library/ast.html), finds where a tag raises an exception, and traces the conditions that lead there. In theory, that works for Django's built-in tags and custom tags in anybody's project.

![Still a work in progress. It doesn't get to take over every evening.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-49.png)

It's still a work in progress. If I had to do all of it by hand at night, I probably wouldn't, to be honest.

I love writing software, but every evening I spend on this is time I'm not spending with my family. Agents let me keep the project moving without letting it take over every evening.

![Before: compare six designs in my head. Now: build six rough versions.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-50.png)

They also let me build rough versions of six possible designs instead of comparing them only in my head. I can run them, read them, and keep the best parts. That gives me better evidence, but the experience is different from working through one version by hand.

![I wrote a Django-template parser in Rust. Compilers, Django internals, a new language. The challenge was part of the fun.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-51.png)

A few years ago I read [_Crafting Interpreters_](https://craftinginterpreters.com/) and wrote a parser for Django templates in Rust.[^django-template-parser] I wanted to learn how compilers worked, understand Django's template engine, and learn a new language. It was a challenge, and the challenge was part of the fun.

[^django-template-parser]: The [first-pass prototype](https://github.com/joshuadavidthomas/django-template-ast) is a separate repository. The [production version](https://github.com/joshuadavidthomas/django-language-server/tree/52b40942237ccf0f51e181f9e7a89f4ef66db87e/crates/djls-templates/src) lives in Django Language Server and remains close to that initial version, but has gone through refinements.

An agent could get surprisingly far on that parser in a few minutes now, but I can still write something by hand when learning it is the point.

![Compiler concepts still apply. Model steering may expire with the next release.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-52.png)

Working with agents is also a kind of puzzle. During a good week, I enjoy finding where one failed, adding a better check, changing the shape of the code, and seeing the next attempt improve.

What frustrates me is how temporary some of that learning is. The compiler concepts I learned still apply. Something I learn about steering one model may stop working when the next one ships. That is frustrating and, sometimes, a little disheartening.

![Cognitive debt: I write code by hand much less often than I used to. I spend more time comparing designs, boundaries, and risks.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-53.png)

There is some [cognitive debt](https://margaretstorey.com/blog/2026/02/09/cognitive-debt/) in that too. I write code by hand much less often than I used to. If these tools disappeared tomorrow, I could still do the work, but it would take me a while to get back to my old speed. That is a real cost.

At the same time, I spend more time comparing designs, thinking about boundaries, reviewing architecture, and deciding which problem is worth solving. I don't know how much of that comes from using agents and how much comes from having six more years of experience.

!["Understand to participate." - Geoffrey Litt](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-54.png)

Geoffrey Litt uses the phrase:

> [Understand to participate.](https://www.geoffreylitt.com/2026/07/02/understanding-is-the-new-bottleneck)

I need to understand the project well enough to decide what happens next instead of handing work to an agent and watching it build something for me.

![Solve the problem. Keep it changeable.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-55.png)

That does not make craftsmanship and building opposites. The business cares whether the application solves its problem, but it also cares when a bug takes three weeks to fix or a feature is too dangerous to change. Clear, changeable code is where those concerns meet.

![That judgement is what remains mine. Disney: is this patch safe enough for this incident? Primary tract: what should the application say already?](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-56.png)

That judgment is what remains mine. At Disney, it meant deciding that a small patch was safe enough for the incident. In the primary-tract refactor, it meant recognizing that the rule belonged on `Lease` instead of in queries and people's memory.

An agent could write the code for either change. I had to decide what the application should say and what risk I was willing to take.

![Get the data structures right. Then the agents can fly.](/static/images/posts/agents-all-the-way-down/slide-57.png)

Getting the data structures right has always mattered. If you're coding with agents, I think it matters even more, because when you get that right, the agents can fly.
